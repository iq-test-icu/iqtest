Historical Figures' IQ Scores: Estimates and Reality

How smart was Albert Einstein? What was Leonardo da Vinci's IQ? People have asked these questions for decades. Because modern intelligence tests were not created until the early 20th century, none of history's greatest scientists, artists, or philosophers ever sat down to take a standardized WAIS or Stanford-Binet exam. Yet, various publications frequently cite exact IQ scores like "160 for Einstein" or "220 for Goethe."

To understand where these numbers come from, we have to look at the academic study of historiometry—specifically the landmark research of psychologist Catharine Cox Miles—and examine the difference between a real test score and a historical estimate.

The Cox Study of 1926

Almost all cited IQ scores for historical figures originate from a single academic source: the 1926 study "The Early Mental Traits of Three Hundred Geniuses," conducted by Catharine Cox (later Catharine Cox Miles) at Stanford University. Working alongside Lewis Terman, the developer of the Stanford-Binet scale, Cox analyzed biographical records, writings, and early developmental milestones of 300 historical geniuses born between 1450 and 1850.

Cox and two independent raters evaluated these records to estimate the age at which each subject performed cognitive tasks that would match the mental age standards of the Stanford-Binet test. The study yielded two estimates for each individual:

  • An IQ estimate based on development up to age 17.
  • An IQ estimate based on development between ages 17 and 26.

The average estimated IQ for the 300 geniuses was 135, but several individuals stood out with exceptionally high scores.

Estimated IQs of Prominent Geniuses

Based on Cox's study and subsequent historiometric reviews by later researchers, here are some of the most frequently discussed historical IQ estimates:

  • John Stuart Mill (Estimated IQ: 190–200): The English philosopher read Greek at age three, Latin at age eight, and wrote a history of Rome at age six. His early intellectual output is considered one of the most extreme cases of early development on record.
  • Johann Wolfgang von Goethe (Estimated IQ: 180–190): The German polymath wrote complex literature and spoke several languages before age ten.
  • Isaac Newton (Estimated IQ: 190–200): Newton formulated calculus and the laws of motion. His logical and mathematical concentration is widely cited as among the highest in human history.
  • Leonardo da Vinci (Estimated IQ: 180–200): Although biographical records from his childhood are sparse, his journals showcase unparalleled spatial reasoning, engineering vision, and artistic synthesis.
  • Marie Curie (Estimated IQ: 180–185): The double Nobel laureate showcased intense focus, logical sorting, and mathematical precision during her early schooling in Poland.
  • Albert Einstein (Estimated IQ: 160): While Einstein did not display the extreme early verbal milestones of John Stuart Mill, his spatial and mathematical thought experiments in adolescence indicate an IQ of at least 160.

Are Historiometric Estimates Accurate?

While Cox’s study was groundbreaking, modern cognitive scientists highlight several major caveats regarding historical IQ estimates:

  1. Biographical Bias: Estimates rely on surviving written records. Individuals from wealthy families who could afford tutors and keep detailed journals are much easier to study than equally bright individuals from impoverished backgrounds.
  2. Mental Age vs. Deviation IQ: Cox used the ratio IQ formula (Mental Age / Chronological Age). Under modern deviation scaling (which compares you strictly against a peer group normal curve), scores above 160 are mathematically extremely rare and difficult to standardize.
  3. The Flynn Effect: Average scores have risen over the generations due to improved education, health, and visual stimulus. A score of 100 today is cognitively different from a score of 100 in 1850.
