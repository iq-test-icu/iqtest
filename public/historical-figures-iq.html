Historical Figures' IQ Scores: Estimates and Reality

Published: August 14, 2026 • Reviewed by: APEX Business Systems Ltd. • Editorial Standards
Almost every famous historical IQ number — from Einstein's 160 to Da Vinci's 200 — is a retrospective biographical estimate, not a recorded test score. Modern intelligence testing was only invented in the early 20th century. What historical records truly preserve is the distinct problem-solving styles and reasoning modes of history's sharpest thinkers.

The origins of historical IQ estimation: Catharine Cox (1926)

The academic origin of estimating historical IQs began with Stanford psychologist Catharine Cox Miles. In 1926, as part of Lewis Terman's monumental Genetic Studies of Genius (Volume II: The Early Mental Traits of Three Hundred Geniuses), Cox and her team analyzed historical records, childhood letters, school achievements, and works of 301 prominent historical figures who lived between 1450 and 1850.

Using historiometric techniques, Cox estimated both childhood and adult IQ scores for figures like Isaac Newton, Leonardo da Vinci, and Johann Wolfgang von Goethe. While pioneering for its time, modern psychometricians emphasize that retrospective estimation is an exploratory academic exercise, not a clinical measurement.

Why retrospective IQ scores are not scientific measurements

Assigning a numerical IQ to someone who lived centuries ago violates core psychometric principles:

  1. Absence of Standardised Testing: None of these individuals sat for timed, supervised psychometric examinations with calibrated control groups.
  2. Historical Survival Bias: Surviving correspondence and published works over-represent successful breakthroughs while obscuring false starts, failures, and mundane cognitive performance.
  3. Conflating Lifelong Achievement with Fluid Intelligence: High achievement in physics or art requires decades of sustained passion, mentorship, and opportunity, which cannot be reduced to raw fluid intelligence points.
