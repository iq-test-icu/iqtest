What is an IQ Test?

An Intelligence Quotient (IQ) test is a psychometrically designed instrument used to assess human intelligence. The resulting score represents a relative measure of an individual's cognitive capabilities compared against a peer group sample. Rather than checking specific factual knowledge, IQ tests attempt to measure fundamental cognitive processes including logical deduction, mathematical reasoning, visual-spatial awareness, and semantic comprehension.

IQ tests have been central to cognitive science, educational diagnostics, and psychology for more than a century. To understand what an IQ test represents, it is helpful to look at its history, how the math is calculated, and what score ranges actually mean in terms of cognitive capacity.

The History of IQ Testing

The modern concept of intelligence testing began in France in the early 20th century. Psychologist Alfred Binet was commissioned by the French government to identify students who struggled academically. Along with Theodore Simon, he created the Binet-Simon scale in 1905. This first test focused on mental age (the average level of performance for a given chronological age).

Later, German psychologist William Stern proposed the term "Intelligence Quotient" (IQ). He calculated it using a simple ratio formula:

IQ = (Mental Age / Chronological Age) * 100

In 1916, Lewis Terman of Stanford University adapted the test for American populations, creating the Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scale. This was the dominant scale until David Wechsler introduced the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) in 1955. Wechsler changed the scoring mechanism from age ratios to deviation IQ, comparing individual performance to a standardized normal distribution of their peers.

How IQ Scores are Calculated

Modern IQ tests do not rely on mental age ratios. Instead, they use a statistical normal distribution (a bell curve) to scale scores. The population mean is set at exactly 100, with a standard deviation (SD) of 15 points. Under this deviation model:

  • 68.2% of the population scores between 85 and 115 (within one standard deviation of the mean).
  • 95.4% of the population scores between 70 and 130 (within two standard deviations).
  • 99.7% of the population scores between 55 and 145 (within three standard deviations).

If you score a 115, you are performing one standard deviation above the mean, placing you in approximately the 84th percentile. A score of 130 places you in the 98th percentile, which is often the threshold for high-intelligence groups like Mensa.

Clinical Tests vs. Online Quizzes

There is a substantial difference between a clinical, psychologist-administered intelligence assessment and a short online quiz:

  1. Standardization and Norming: Clinical assessments (such as the WAIS-IV or Woodcock-Johnson) are administered in controlled environments by licensed professionals. The results are compared against representative national samples.
  2. Breadth of Domains: A full clinical test covers multiple categories over several hours, including working memory, processing speed, perceptual reasoning, and verbal comprehension. Online quizzes typically assess logical patterns and sequences over 5 to 15 minutes.
  3. Reliability and Validity: Clinical instruments are psychometrically validated over years of research to ensure they measure general intelligence (the "g factor") reliably without cultural or demographic bias.

Online quizzes like IQ·Test are great tools for entertainment and personal reflection, but they are not clinical diagnostics. They provide a quick, engaging snapshot of puzzle-solving performance using the 85–145 deviation frame of reference purely as a familiar scaling metric.

What do the ranges mean?

Under the standard Wechsler scale, scores are classified into general ranges:

  • 130 and above: Very Superior (often referred to as gifted).
  • 120 to 129: Superior.
  • 110 to 119: High Average.
  • 90 to 109: Average (the bulk of the general population).
  • 80 to 89: Low Average.
  • 70 to 79: Borderline.
  • 69 and below: Extremely Low.

IQ is not a fixed, immutable measure of your worth or capability. It is a model of logical and spatial processing speeds. Research shows that cognitive abilities can shift throughout life based on education, lifestyle, and health.
